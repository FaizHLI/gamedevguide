A learning plan for solo developers

Making Games Solo

A three-year path through game design, Godot, drawing, pixel art, and music, written for a programmer who can code but has not yet finished a game.

This is a complete plan for learning to make games on your own. It covers five crafts in parallel: design, the Godot engine, drawing, pixel art, and music. It assumes you can already program and have not yet shipped a finished game.

The plan is built around one rule: you learn by finishing. Each stage ends with a small game released in public, however rough. Skill comes from shipping, not from study alone.

~3 years at a steady pace 5 crafts learned together 15+ small games shipped before the big one

How to use this plan

The most common mistake is trying to learn everything at once while building the one large game you have wanted to make for years. That game needs skills you do not have yet, so it stalls or collapses under its own weight. Build it in year three, not now. A small game that you finish teaches you more in a weekend than a large one studied for a month.

Each stage ends with something released, even when the art is crude and the music is thin. Finishing is a habit, and few people build it. Without it, the rest is only practice for a game that never ships.

The five crafts advance in parallel, on purpose. Drawing starts with a pen and printer paper and takes the longest to pay off. Music builds slowly. Design needs theory and then testing against real players. Godot will come fastest, because you already think like a programmer, so use it to finish games while the other four catch up.

The right mental model
You are learning five crafts at once
Design, programming, drawing, music, and project management are each large skills on their own. You will be a beginner in all five for a while. The goal is not mastery but enough of each to finish one small, coherent game. Programming is your head start, so spend it on finishing while the other four catch up.
Breadth first, depth second
In year one, finish six to ten tiny complete games, each with its own art and sound rather than borrowed placeholders. Shipping is a skill you build by repetition. Ten small finished games teach more about how a game feels than a stack of books.
Keep practice separate from projects
Keep your studies apart from your games. Keep a sketchbook, a folder of unfinished music loops, and Godot experiments that go nowhere. Mixing practice into a real project tends to harm both.
I
The core craft

Game design

Design is the craft of shaping what a player feels using rules alone. Many beginners skip it and then cannot work out why their finished game feels lifeless.

Section 1.1
Theory foundations

Exercises
01
After each game you play, write a 200-word breakdown: the core mechanic, what it teaches, the feedback loop, and what it did well and badly.
Most people play without analyzing. This makes you do both at once.
02
Take a simple game such as chess, Tetris, or Flappy Bird and write a full design document for it from scratch, as if you were inventing it: core loop, goals, feedback, and win and loss conditions.
03
Break down three games in different genres: a puzzle, a platformer, and a roguelike. For each, identify the core loop, the skill it builds, and the moment-to-moment feedback.
Studying across genres prevents tunnel vision. Most developers only study the kind of game they already want to make.
Section 1.2
Applied design: ideation and the one-page GDD
▾
GoalThe habit of turning an idea into a written document. Design improves through output, not through underlining other people's books.
The one-page GDD
For each small game, write one page first: the core feeling (what does the player feel?), the core loop (what happens every thirty seconds?), the win and loss conditions, one mechanic that stands out, and an honest estimate of scope. Then cut the scope in half.
Community
Ludum Dare / GMTK Game Jam
Enter at least one jam every three months. A 48-hour deadline teaches more than months of reading. Your first jam game will be poor, and you will still learn a lot from it.
FreeDo this
Book (later)
Rules of Play — Salen & Zimmerman
The academic end of design theory, and dense. Open it only after you have shipped three or four games and want precise language for systems.
~$50
Ongoing exercises
01
Keep a weekly mechanic journal. Once a week, describe one mechanic you invented or noticed: how would you build it, and what behavior does it reward?
02
Take a mechanic you like and invert it. Generate five variants in twenty minutes without filtering. This is ideation, not judgment.
03
Design a game with one constraint: playable in under three minutes, no tutorial, and clear to a stranger on the first try. Write the page, build it in Godot, and watch one real person play it.
It forces you to teach through levels and visuals instead of text that players skip.

II
Your fastest track

Godot

This will be your easiest track, since you already program. GDScript is close to Python. The harder shift is the node-and-scene model, which is not the class hierarchy you are used to. Get that right and the rest is straightforward.

Engine fundamentals

Core concepts, in order
  • Nodes and scenes: the building blocks. A scene is a reusable tree of nodes. Learn it on its own terms rather than mapping it onto class inheritance.
  • Signals: Godot's event system and the key to decoupled code. They replace most direct calls between nodes.
  • GDScript: start untyped, then add type hints by month two. Static typing catches mistakes earlier and documents intent.
  • Physics bodies: CharacterBody2D, RigidBody2D, and StaticBody2D each have a purpose. Using the wrong one is a common beginner mistake.
  • AnimationTree: use AnimationPlayer for simple cases and AnimationTree with a state machine for characters with several states.
  • Autoloads (singletons): for game state, audio, and save/load. The standard way to share data between scenes.
  • Custom Resources: an underused way to do data-driven design for items, stats, and dialogue. Learn them by month three.
Exercises
01
Build Pong from scratch, with no tutorial, in one afternoon. This is your baseline. If it is hard, do the GDQuest fundamentals first.
02
Build a CharacterBody2D controller: 8-directional movement, an idle/walk state machine, an Area2D pickup using signals, and a HUD that updates on pickup. Use Godot primitives, no art. The point is the architecture.
This covers most of what a basic 2D game needs.
03
Build a save/load system through an Autoload: player position, a score, and one boolean, written to a JSON file and loaded at startup. Most tutorials skip this.

Game feel: the techniques that matter
  • Coyote time: allow a jump for about 0.1–0.15s after the player leaves an edge. Every platformer should do this.
  • Input buffering: queue a jump up to about 0.1s before landing, so inputs are not dropped.
  • Screen shake: apply it through a Camera2D offset, not a viewport transform.
  • Hitstop: freeze for 3–8 frames on impact using Engine.time_scale. It adds weight to a hit.
  • Order of effects: add juice in this order: particles, sound, shake, hitstop. Finish each layer before the next.
  • Tweens: Godot 4's Tween API is good for UI, camera zoom, and score popups. Avoid using it for motion that should feel physical.
Exercises
01
"Juice it or lose it": take a plain prototype and add screen shake, a particle burst on pickup, sound, an animated score popup, and a death animation. Compare before and after. This is the clearest single exercise for game feel.
02
Build an enemy with a three-state machine: patrol, chase, attack. Route damage through an Autoload signal bus rather than direct node references.
03
Build a small dungeon generator using binary space partitioning, drawn with a TileMap. No gameplay needed; the goal is to think procedurally.

III
The slowest track

Drawing & pixel art

Art starts with a pen and paper, not with Aseprite. Drawabox builds the unglamorous foundations: spatial reasoning, line confidence, and the ability to picture a form in three dimensions before you draw it. These carry directly into pixel art, because once you understand a form in space you can render it in a 16×16 grid. This is the slowest track here and the one most people try to skip. Do not skip it.
Section 3.1
Drawing fundamentals (Drawabox)
▾
GoalSpatial reasoning, a confident line, and construction. Lessons 0–3 and the 250 Box Challenge in year one; Lessons 4–7 continuing through year two.
The 50% rule. Spend half your drawing time drawing for fun, not on exercises. This matters. The exercises build skill; the play keeps you coming back. Ignore it and you are likely to quit.
Course
Drawabox
Free, structured, construction-based drawing fundamentals: seven lessons and several challenges. Pen and paper only, with no pencil and no undo. Start here, before any pixel art.
FreeStart here
Supplies
Fineliners + printer paper
0.5mm pigment liners or any felt-tip, on printer paper rather than a sketchbook. The pen forces you to commit to each mark, which is the point.
~$10Essential
The Drawabox lessons
  • Lesson 0, getting started: mindset, tools, and the 50% rule. Read it before you start.
  • Lesson 1, lines, ellipses, boxes: superimposed and ghosted lines, ghosted planes, ellipse tables, funnels, and rough perspective. The foundation for everything after. Draw from the shoulder, not the wrist.
  • 250 Box Challenge: between Lessons 1 and 2. 250 boxes in three-point perspective, checking convergence on each. It takes weeks, and it is where spatial reasoning starts to click. Do not rush or skip it.
  • Lesson 2, contour, texture, detail: contour on organic forms, texture as cast shadow rather than surface noise, and implicit versus explicit detail.
  • Lesson 3, construction with plants: building complex forms from simple ones. Drawing from imagination starts to mean something here.
  • Lessons 4–7 (year two): insects, animals, everyday objects, and vehicles. The same reasoning applied to harder subjects. Continue through year two alongside pixel art.
Warmups, a permanent habit
Daily
10–15 minutes of Lesson 1 exercises before each session: superimposed lines, ghosted lines, ghosted planes, ellipse tables, and funnels.
Early
Finish Lesson 1. Ghost every line before drawing it.
Then
The 250 Box Challenge. Five to ten boxes per session, checking convergence after each. It feels tedious; the tedium is the skill forming.
After
Lessons 2–3 at your own pace: contour, texture from reference, and plant construction. By Lesson 3 you are drawing real things from observation.
Section 3.2
Pixel art fundamentals
▾

Core techniques, in order
  • Readability first: at 16×16, every pixel serves the silhouette. If you cannot name the object from its outline, the design has failed, however nice the detail. Your Drawabox construction tells you what matters before you place a pixel.
  • Manual anti-aliasing: place intermediate pixels to soften edges. Learn it early; hard jagged edges mark a beginner.
  • Color ramps: each base color needs light, mid, dark, and shadow values. Build them by shifting hue, not only by sliding brightness.
  • Dithering: alternating pixels to suggest an in-between color. Learn checkerboard dithering first.
  • Tiles: they must repeat seamlessly. Avoid high-contrast patterns that reveal the grid.
Pixel practice
First
Basic objects at 16×16 — rock, tree, chest, sword, potion — using three colors each. Aim only for a clear silhouette.
Next
Redo them with real color ramps (four or more values) and light from the top-left. Then draw a simple humanoid at 16×16 and 32×32.
Then
A 4-tile ground set — grass, dirt, stone, water — that tiles seamlessly, then a dungeon set. Construction makes environments easier to reason about.
Study
Recreate a sprite from a game you admire, pixel by pixel. Do three to five. Taking professional work apart reveals techniques you would not find otherwise.

▾

Animation, in order
01
A bouncing ball, 8 frames, with squash on landing. Practice weight, timing, and squash and stretch.
02
A 4-frame idle with breathing and a weight shift. More frames is not better; four well-timed frames beat twelve sloppy ones.

Environment and UI skills
  • UI components: health bar, inventory slot, dialogue frame, and button states (normal, hover, pressed). Build a reusable set in your game's palette.
  • Style guides: a locked palette, character proportions, tile scale, outline rules, and shading direction. Write one before any multi-week project. Aim for this by year three.
  • Portraits: 48×48 or 64×64 faces with two or three expressions each. At that size, the eyebrows and mouth carry the emotion.
IV
Most often neglected

Music & sound

Audio is the craft solo developers neglect most, and it adds more to a game than they expect. Most reach for a free asset pack or drop in two tracks and move on. Do better than that. Learn sound effects before music, and theory before the DAW. The software is useless if you cannot hear what you are aiming for.
Section 4.1

▾
GoalWorking sound in every game from day one, made with tools that need almost no skill.

Free
Godot audio, learned early
  • AudioStreamPlayer vs 2D: the 2D version attenuates with distance. Use 2D for in-world sounds and the plain player for UI and music.
  • Audio buses: set up Master → Music and Master → SFX from the start of every project. Never hardcode a volume.
  • Pitch randomization: shift pitch_scale by ±0.1 on each SFX. It removes the machine-gun effect of repeated identical sounds. One line, large effect.
  • AudioStreamRandomizer: a Godot 4 resource that cycles through clips for footsteps, hits, and other frequent sounds.

FreeStart here
YouTube

Analysis of game music itself, where theory meets practice. It explains why well-known soundtracks work harmonically.
FreeEssential
YouTube

Deeper theory. Watch two or three a week, especially the videos on rhythm, harmony, and modal interchange.
Free
Theory, in order
  • Notes, scales, keys: major, natural minor, and pentatonic. Understand intervals. This is the floor.
  • Chords: major, minor, diminished, and augmented triads, then dominant, major, and minor 7ths.
  • Progressions: Roman-numeral analysis. Why I–IV–V–I resolves, and common progressions like I–V–vi–IV. Learn them by ear as well as by sight.
  • Rhythm and time: 4/4, 3/4, 6/8, syncopation, and changing tempo between game states without a jolt.
  • Melody: tension and release, writing a motif and developing it, and AABA form.
  • Modes: Dorian (dark but not sad, as in Minecraft), Lydian (dreamlike, as in Kondo's work), and Mixolydian (adventurous). These give soundtracks their emotional character.
Section 4.3
Composing for games: loops and adaptive music
▾
GoalShort, seamlessly looping tracks for different game states, and an understanding of how game music differs from other music.
Game music loops indefinitely, plays under other sounds, and should not draw attention to itself. It has to hold a mood for long stretches without tiring the listener. Work within that constraint rather than against it.

Composition
01
A 30-second seamlessly looping exploration track: no audible seam, not so interesting that it distracts from play, with a clear mood. Use three or four instruments.

V
The hardest part

Finishing & shipping

Finishing is a separate skill from building, and the rarer of the two. Most solo developers collect abandoned prototypes. Avoiding that takes systems, not willpower.

Section 5.1
The scope contract
▾
The halving rule
Whatever scope you think a game needs, cut it in half before starting. Of the features that feel essential, remove half. You will underestimate complexity for years. After about ten shipped games you will judge it better; until then, assume your estimate is too large.
The prototype test
Before committing, build the core mechanic as a grey box in under two hours. If it is not at least interesting in that plain form, art and music will not save it. Cut ideas early and often.
The vertical slice
Before other content, build one complete, polished slice: one level, one enemy, and one mechanic, with final art and audio, releasable as a demo on its own. Everything else grows from there. It prevents the state where everything is 80% done and nothing ships.
Project management for one
  • A simple kanban: To Do, In Progress, Done. Keep no more than three items in progress at once.
  • Two-week sprints: each with a concrete deliverable, not "work on enemy AI" but "the enemy detects, chases, and damages the player." Testable and binary.
  • A "maybe later" list: when a new feature idea arrives mid-project, write it down and do not build it. Most of these ideas are distractions.
  • A ship date set before you start: a real date on the calendar, not "when it's done." A game with no date tends not to ship.

Your default for everything. Free to list, and it supports web (HTML5), Windows, Mac, and Linux. Jams run here. Release everything, including the weak games.

Playtest before you release
  • Five people, five tests: the minimum before any release. Watch in silence and explain nothing. Note every place a player gets lost.
  • The first 60 seconds: if a stranger cannot tell what to do within a minute and without a tutorial, the opening is wrong. Most players quit an itch.io game in under two minutes.
VI
What to do, and roughly when

Production calendar

Rather than a rigid week-by-week schedule, this is the work in order, broken into stages, each with an estimate in focused hours. Set how many hours you can give per week and the plan estimates how long the whole thing takes. The estimate is only as honest as the number you enter.

Set your pace

How many focused hours can you give this in a normal week? Use a realistic number, not an aspirational one.

or hrs/week
Read the stages in order, but within a stage do the work in whatever sequence fits your week; the five crafts run in parallel. The hours below are focused hours. The estimate next to each stage is those hours measured against the pace you set.
STAGE 1 · YEAR ONE
Setup, First Tools, Ship Pong
~40 focused hrs
The goalinstall everything, build Pong from scratch, and put it on itch.io. Begin Drawabox and a weekly music-theory habit. Pong ships with crude art — what matters here is Godot, the foundations, and the act of finishing one thing.
The work, in rough order
  • Godot: install it. GDQuest beginner episodes 1–8. Then build Pong with no tutorial — two paddles, ball, wall collisions, scoring, using primitive shapes.
  • Art: buy fineliners and paper. Read Drawabox Lesson 0, then work through Lesson 1 — superimposed and ghosted lines, ghosted planes, ellipse tables, funnels. Open Aseprite and make a paddle, a ball, a number font (0–9) at 8×8. The art is crude. Ship it.
  • Music: work the first ~16 musictheory.net lessons. In BFXR, make four sounds — ball hit, wall bounce, score, game over. Open LMMS and get a C-major scale out of it.
  • Design: read "A Theory of Fun" cover to cover. Write two 200-word design autopsies.
  • Ship: export Pong as HTML5, make an itch.io account, upload, write three sentences, take a screenshot, and publish it. Then write a short retrospective.
One game live on itch.io. Drawabox Lesson 1 done, the 250 Box Challenge begun. musictheory.net a quarter through. "A Theory of Fun" read. The Pong art is basic — which is exactly correct for now.
STAGE 2 · YEAR ONE
Build & Ship a Platformer
~46 focused hrs
The goala complete platformer with your own art, sound, and a basic music loop, shipped. Keep grinding the boxes and begin pixel art in earnest. Start "The Art of Game Design."
The work
  • Godot: write the one-page GDD first. A CharacterBody2D controller — run, jump, gravity, ground detection, coyote time. A patrolling enemy, Area2D collectibles via signals, a HUD with score and lives. Three short TileMap levels (one teaches jumping, one the enemy, one combines them), death, and level transitions. Title and win/lose screens, audio buses, pitch randomization on the pickup. Export and ship it.
  • Art: 250 Box Challenge to ~80 boxes, with Lesson 1 warmups first every time. Begin pixel art: BJG's beginner guide; five 16×16 objects in three colors each; a 16×16 player; a 3–4 tile ground set; a simple background. Use one palette (DB32 or PICO-8).
  • Music: musictheory.net lessons ~17–28 (intervals, triads, progressions). In LMMS, grow a 4-bar melody into a 16-bar loop with a bass line and a kick-snare pattern; export as .ogg. Five BFXR sounds for the platformer.
  • Design: "Art of Game Design" through ~chapter 9. Apply a lens or two to your platformer. Watch a stranger play your three levels without a word of help, and note where they stall.
Two games shipped. A first platformer with original (simple) art, sound, and music. ~80 boxes. 10+ pixel objects. musictheory.net fundamentals done. "Art of Game Design" nine chapters in.

New Genre, First Jam, Static Sprites
~52 focused hrs
The goalbuild in a genre you've never touched, and enter your first game jam. Sprites are still static — no animation yet.
The work
  • Godot: a one-page GDD for a top-down game — shooter, puzzle, or RPG exploration. 8-directional movement, one interaction mechanic, camera follow. The goallevel up your Godot architecture, build a game that lives or dies on feel, finish the 250 Box Challenge, and read "Game Feel." Your construction sense is about to start paying you back in pixels.
The work

STAGE 16 · YEAR THREE
Production — Build the Game
~500 focused hrs
The goalbuild the full game in two-week sprints — vertical slice first, then expansion — on the back of two years of practice, with a weekly devlog. This is the thing you've been building toward. Don't flinch now.
The work
  • Foundation: define the full architecture — scene tree, signal graph, data flow — and build the production project from your template (player controller, camera, UI, save/load, audio buses). Then the player character at final quality, all states, all directions: your benchmark.
  • Vertical slice: one complete level with final art, animation, audio, and juice — demo-releasable on its own. Polish it, playtest with five, fix what breaks, post a devlog with a gameplay GIF.
  • Content sprints: two-week sprints, each with a testable deliverable; build all remaining content, reusing assets aggressively. At the midpoint, play the whole game start to finish and cut anything that isn't working — the last honest chance to descope. Then feature freeze: only bug fixes, tuning, polish, and audio after that.
  • Polish & test: particles, shaders, transitions, a final art-consistency pass. Playtest with 5–8 (devs and non-devs), and triage ruthlessly: game-breaking > confusing > annoying > nice-to-have. Final audio mix.
The game is content-complete, feature-frozen, and tested by 10+ people. All art and audio original, backed by 2+ years of practice. A devlog with 20+ entries. You are ready to ship the thing you weren't able to make three years ago.

~270 focused hrs
The goalrelease it publicly, build the marketing around it, consider Steam if it's earned, and write the retrospective that closes the arc.
The work
  • Press kit: 6–10 polished screenshots, a 30–60s trailer (OBS, your best gameplay, your best music), descriptions at 50/150/500 words, a factsheet.
  • Launch: finalize the itch page (Web, Windows, Mac, screenshots, trailer, credits, price). If Steam, submit the page and build now ($100). VII
How projects stall

Failure modes

Each of these has derailed people with real promise. Read them as warnings.

Tutorial hell
Following tutorials endlessly and building nothing from scratch. After each tutorial, close it and rebuild the same thing from memory, then build something similar without help. The move from following to creating is where skill forms, and where most people stop.
The dream game
Attempting your masterpiece as one of your first projects. It needs skills you do not have yet, so it stalls or accumulates technical debt until it collapses. The polished game is year three. Until then, ship small and medium games that build toward it.
Skipping fundamentals
Jumping to pixel art without form, construction, and spatial reasoning. The constraints of pixel art hide the gap for a while, since you can make a passable 16×16 sprite knowing little, but it returns the moment you attempt a character, an environment, or animation. Drawabox is slow because it builds something lasting. Without it, there is a low ceiling on your art.
Tool switching
Moving from Godot to Unity to Unreal, each seeming to offer what the last lacked. None of them deliver it. Every switch costs two to four weeks of relearning and ships nothing. Commit to Godot for three years before looking elsewhere.
Asset-store dependency
Buying art and audio instead of making it, which stops those crafts from improving. Asset packs are fine for jams and prototypes. For anything you release, the work should be your own.
Passive consumption
Watching videos about making games instead of making them. Spend at least two hours practicing for every hour of content. If you have watched more game-dev content this month than you have spent in Godot or Aseprite, you are not learning.
Skipping music
Treating audio as optional. A silent game gets fewer plays and less feedback. Use BFXR and one looping track from day one, however rough. Bad audio is better than none.
